Simplicity

Credit goes to John Amash for imparting me with these insights and metaphors.

Complexity in software or automation systems is the death of productivity: simplicity is key.

Simplicity — in the software solutions and automation sense — is ironically complex to define and may seem initially unintuitive, but here goes.

Definition

A simple solution is digestible and understandable, or low-concept: the lower the number of variables and unknown factors, the more simple the solution is. Conversely, a complex solution introduces variables and unknown outcomes to a system that can result in errors and lost tribal knowledge long term.

Programmers and solutions architects often add variables and layers of abstraction to make managing our work easier. This is generally good, but when the levels of abstraction lead to the system flow being difficult to know and understand, it can become a problem. A complex solution with many levels of abstraction obfuscating lower level details will present itself as the easy path because abstraction can hide complexity, but that doesn't mean it's the best solution to implement in the long term. A simple solution can appear sluggish and cumbersome, but is often better to implement if it can meet business requirements, especially if it's easy to understand and to make changes to the process without side effects.

Simplicity vs Difficulty

Simple solutions are hard work, and complex solutions are complex to ease that burden, but complexity comes with trade-offs. This is best explained using a metaphor: digging a deep hole with a shovel is simple in concept, yet hard manual work. All you need is a shovel and elbow grease. It's easy to understand, the path to completion is linear, and there aren't many variables to juggle. Digging a deep hole with complex construction equipment is much easier with far less manual work, but the process has many more variables and therefore much more could go wrong. Transporting large machinery is difficult. Each machine could fail in various different ways. A backhoe could get stuck in the mud. You could accidentally rupture a gas or sewer line that you wouldn't have if you were using a shovel.

Automation is not always the answer as it can lead to added complexity. That's not to say, however, that adding complexity is never the answer. Each automated solution should be a cost-benefit analysis, weighing the risks of adding more variables and outcomes to a system.

Practical Example

A client (customer, coworker, etc.) comes to you with a problem: they find it time consuming and draining to wade through social media comments to find out what user impressions of new products are. A software developer's first instinct is to say "how can I solve this problem for them using automation?" You instantly start thinking of complex automated solutions: "I could build a web scraper that scans multiple different social media platforms, feeds that data into an LLM that we train on social media impressions data, and spits out a response based on the impressions it finds!" That's all well and good, and it makes the client's life easier, but it introduces an exorbitantly complex system to do so while abstracting away a large portion of their job. It would drain so many resources to develop the system, train the model, and maintain the system, that it's probably better to retort and tell them to continue with the simple solution of looking through social media feeds themselves. There's probably a larger problem underneath the surface resulting in this person's plate being too full such that they don't feel like they have time or resources to do their job properly. Maybe the better solution is to hire more real people to split the task load instead of replacing the employee with AI.

Illustration: LLMs are Complex

The most extreme modern illustration of these added variables incurring complexity is AI, specifically Large Language Models (LLMs). LLMs are build with millions or billions of variables (the values of which are determined by huge sets of underlying training data), and are therefore impossible to understand fully by a human. This makes them incredibly complex, as it's impossible to determine what the LLM will say in response to an input. An input can lead to many responses, i.e. LLMs are not functions in the mathematical sense. This is the fundamental problem that results in hallucinations and the models saying things that the engineers who made the model never intended. LLMs contain so much information in their training data and so many different variables that it's impossible for them to decipher the truth in their own responses.

The scariest thing is that LLMs make our lives so much easier because of this complexity. This is the source of my strong skepticism for AI. I don't want to put my trust in something that cannot be fully understood by a human due to its complexity. Using an LLM for programming may seem "productive" because you can produce so much more code, but because code and automation incurs complexity, it may not be as productive for the system as it seems. When you offload work to an LLM, the LLM won't tell you that your assumptions are wrong, it'll try to explain around and find solutions within those incorrect assumptions. AI will be your yes-man while also gaslighting you; it'll let you manipulate yourself.

Productivity and Agility

Being able to be productive in a software system means being able to confidently and quickly make changes to the system to meet a company's or a client's needs. When you can't be confident or be quick in making changes to the system, that's usually a result of the system being complex.

Keeping software systems simple also keeps them agile. Simple things are easy to change because they're easy to understand. Software systems are usually a network or a graph of interacting pieces that need to communicate. Each node can connect to various other nodes, so each added node can explode the number of connections (variables). You can be much more confident making a change to a node or a variable in a system when there's 3 nodes rather than if there's a hundred because it doesn't take much time or effort to know and understand the whole system when the amount of variables is low. Having to context-load and unravel the tangled web of an complex existing automated system is time intensive, resource intensive, and stressful for the person making the changes. As a simple ;) rule of thumb, only automate the things that are taking up significant man-hours or that are error-prone when done manually.

Closing Remarks

Programmers are often too eager to use our skills that we sometimes use them without necessity. I've fallen victim to it myself. We build tools that help us abstract away the nitty-gritty that sometimes is better to leave bare. We have the magical power of software and logic at our fingertips. We lie in wait like ambush predators, ready and eager to use our skills to squash problems. The result is that we focus on pushing code instead of actual long-term productivity for the client or company we're working for. We develop and construct beautiful software solutions even when they introduce unnecessary complexity.

Software and automation are powerful tools, but power always needs to be used responsibly, with discernment, and only when needed.
