Simplicity

Credit goes to John Amash for imparting me with these insights and metaphors.

Complexity in software or automation systems is the death of productivity: simplicity is key.

Simplicity — in the software automation and solutions sense that I'm referring to — is ironically complex to define and may seem initially unintuitive, but here goes.

A simple solution refers to how digestible and understandable, or how low-concept a solution is: the lower the number of variables and unknown factors, the more simple the solution is. Conversely, a complex solution introduces variables and unknown outcomes to a system that can result in errors and lost tribal knowledge long term. Programmers and solutions architects often add variables and abstraction to make our lives easier. This is generally good, but when the levels of abstraction lead to the system flow being difficult to know and understand it becomes a problem. A complex solution with many levels of abstraction obfuscating lower level details will present itself as the easy path, but that doesn't mean it's the best solution to implement in the long term; a simple solution can appear sluggish and cumbersome, but is often better to implement long term. That is, especially if the benefits of ease of understanding and the ability to make changes quickly and without side effects outweighs the added time and manual effort of maintaining the simple solution.

Simple solutions are usually hard work, and complex solutions are complex to ease that burden, but complexity comes with trade-offs. This is best explained using a metaphor: digging a deep hole with a shovel is simple in concept, yet hard manual work. All you need is a shovel and elbow grease. It's easy to understand, the path to completion is linear, and there aren't many variables to juggle. Digging a deep hole with complex construction equipment is much easier and less manual work, but the process has many more variables and therefore much more could go wrong. Transporting large machinery is difficult. Each machine could fail in various different ways. A backhoe could get stuck in the mud. You could accidentally rupture a gas or sewer line that you wouldn't have if you were using a shovel.

Automation is not always the answer as it can lead to added complexity. That's not to say, however, that adding complexity is never the answer. Each automated solution should be a cost-benefit analysis, weighing the risks of adding more variables and outcomes to a system.

The best and most extreme modern illustration of these added variables incurring complexity is AI, specifically Large Language Models (LLMs). LLMs are build with millions of variables, and are therefore impossible to understand fully by a human. This makes them incredibly complex, as it's impossible to determine what the LLM will say in response to an input. This is the fundamental problem that results in hallucinations and the models saying things that the architects never intended. LLMs contain so much information in their training data and so many different variables that it's impossible for them to decipher the truth in their responses.

The scariest thing is that LLMs make our lives so much easier because of this complexity. This is the source of my strong skepticism for AI. I don't want to put my trust in something that cannot be fully understood by a human because it's so complex. Using an LLM for programming may seem "productive" because you can produce so much more code, but because code and automation incurs complexity, it may not be as productive for the system as it seems.
